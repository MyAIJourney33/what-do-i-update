Privacy and terms

Last updated November 21, 2026

The short version
  • No account, no tracking, no ads and no cookies.
  • We count how many phones open the app each day: just numbers, never who. You can turn it off.
  • The list of things you own stays on your phone. We never see it, store it or sell it.
  • We never send download links. Any message with a link that claims to be from us is a scam.
  • We'll never email you first. We only reply to emails you send to help@whatdoiupdate.com.
  • Instant alerts are optional. If you turn them on, we keep only an anonymous push address.

What stays on your phone

Your device lists, any names you add (like "Mom"), your checkmarks, the alerts you mark as fixed, which alerts you've already seen (so new ones get a New label) and the breached services you mark as ones you use. They're saved in your browser's storage on this phone and nowhere else. To remove them, go to Settings › Erase everything on this phone.

The backup code in Settings is made on your phone. It only goes where you paste it.

What we send you

Everyone gets the same public files: the list of flaws being attacked, our news notes, support dates and a list of known data breaches. Your phone compares them with your list. That's why we never need to know what you own.

What we count

To know whether the app is helping anyone, we keep a few daily totals. Counting isn't tracking: we never know who opened the app, only how many phones did.

  • Phones that opened the app. At most once a day, your phone sends our alert server a blank "opened today" signal. The server adds 1 to that day's total.
  • Phones watching someone else's devices. The same signal says yes or no to one question: does this phone have more than one list (family watch)? That adds 1 to a second total.
  • Instant alert sign-ups. The number of push addresses we hold (see above). Just the number.

The signal has no ID, no cookie and nothing about what's on your lists. Like any web request it arrives with your IP address; we don't keep it. The totals are kept by Cloudflare, which runs our alert server, as one row of numbers per day. They aren't secret, and we may share them.

To stop counting this phone, go to Settings › Daily count › Don't count this phone. If your browser sends Global Privacy Control or Do Not Track, we never count it.

Links to our sources

News stories and alerts link to where the information came from (news sites, device makers and government agencies) so you can read more or check our work. These links only appear under "Read more" and show the site's name. They open the other site in a new tab, and that site's own privacy rules apply. We don't track which links you tap. Alerts and notifications never contain links, and we never link to downloads: updates always come from your device's own Settings.

What our web host sees

The app is hosted on GitHub Pages. Like most web hosts, GitHub may keep technical logs, including visitors' IP addresses, to keep its service secure. We don't receive those logs or use them. GitHub's own privacy statement covers them.

Instant alerts (only if you turn them on)

When you turn on instant alerts, your browser makes a push address: a random web address from Apple, Google, Mozilla or Microsoft that lets us send a notification to this browser. We save only that address. It doesn't include your name, phone number, email or anything you own.

The addresses are kept by Cloudflare, which runs our alert server. About once an hour the server checks for new Do now alerts. When there is one, it sends every address the same empty ping, and your phone shows "New alert — open the app." The app then checks whether that alert is about something you own. Sometimes it won't be.

When you turn alerts off in Settings, we delete your address. We also delete it when the browser maker tells us it no longer works. Like other web hosts, Cloudflare may keep technical logs, including IP addresses, to keep its service secure. We don't use those logs.

Where the information comes from

  • The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's (CISA) list of flaws known to be used in attacks.
  • Makers' security notices and news reports. Every news note names its source.
  • Support end dates from endoflife.date.
  • Breach information from Have I Been Pwned.
